# Weather and seasons

How to use this: rules of thumb for planning around weather. Pass dates are typical windows only—never say a road is open or closed without the official source, and prefer a live forecast over any average here.

## Hard stops

- For a motorcycle, the only conditions that call off a riding day are snow, ice, and tornadoes. Rain, cold within the limits below, wind, and heat are suited up for. That is about the day, not the hour: within a day, still wait out lightning and hail, flooded washes, dust storms, and severe crosswinds, as described below.
- Snow or ice on the road means a motorcycle does not go. Chain controls do not address motorcycles at all (a bike cannot be chained), but an active chain control is a strong sign of snow or ice, so treat that stretch as impassable on two wheels. A car follows the control's own R1, R2, or R3 rules.
- A car can drive in conditions a motorcycle cannot. In a mixed group, the bikes decide.

## Cold (motorcycle)

For a long day in the saddle, heated gear or not:

- 50s °F: brisk. Below about 50°F for any extended stretch, riders want a heated vest.
- 40s: very cold. Flag any stretch forecast in the 40s.
- 30s: doable only for short periods. A route that runs into the evening as temperatures fall into the 30s is debilitating; warn hard.
- Below 32°F: not a riding day.

Wind chill at speed makes every band worse than the number suggests. Using the National Weather Service formula at 65 mph: 50°F feels like about 39, 45°F like about 32, 40°F like about 24, and 35°F like about 17. The formula is for bare skin, so good gear helps and wet gear makes it worse; use it to compare, not as a reading. Add any headwind to road speed.

Cold first takes away fine control—clutch and brake feel, then judgment. Shivering, fumbling hands, slurred speech, or confusion means stop and warm up now.

Bicycles generate their own heat but descend fast; long descents after a climb are where cyclists get cold. Cars are not limited by cold except on snow and ice.

## Elevation

- Air cools roughly 3.5°F per 1,000 feet of climb on average; it varies with moisture and fails in valley inversions at dawn.
- Example: leaving a 90°F valley at 1,000 feet to cross a 10,000-foot pass is about 31°F cooler at the top—around 59°F before wind chill. A comfortable valley morning can mean a near-freezing summit.
- Snow is possible in any month on passes above about 8,000 feet in the Rockies, Sierra, and Cascades. A summer route over a high pass needs a fallback.
- Prefer a point forecast for the summit when one exists.

## Heat and hydration

- Riders sweat more than they notice because the wind evaporates it. Drink more water than seems necessary, sip continuously, and replace electrolytes on hot days.
- About 0.5–1 liter per hour is a common working figure, nearer 1 liter in 100°F+ desert. These are working numbers, not medical ones.
- In desert summer, start early and plan to be off the road or in shade through the hottest hours (roughly early afternoon to late afternoon). Keep fuel and water stops closer together.
- Heat exhaustion: heavy sweating, pale clammy skin, cramps, weakness, dizziness, headache, nausea. Stop at the next exit and cool down; do not ride through it.
- Heat stroke is an emergency: confusion, very high body temperature. Call 911 and cool the person.
- A rider who feels oddly cold in the heat, or oddly hot in the cold, should speak up and stop.
- Evaporative cooling vests work well in dry heat and much less in humid air.
- Bicycles: heat is the bigger risk than for any other vehicle; ride early and carry more water than the gap seems to need.

## Rain

- Rain alone is not a reason to cancel; it is a reason to slow down and wear rain gear.
- The first rain after a dry spell lifts oil and is especially slick, most of all in the first stretch of a storm.
- Painted lines, metal plates, manhole covers, tar snakes, and steel bridge decks are slippery when wet. Shaded corners stay damp longer.

## Mountain thunderstorms

- In summer, mountain storms usually build in the early afternoon, though they can come at any time.
- Plan to be over exposed high-alpine sections (above treeline) by late morning in July and August. Lightning and hail on an exposed pass are serious hazards on a motorcycle; do not shelter at overlooks, fences, or lone trees.

## Southwest monsoon

- The National Weather Service defines the monsoon as June 15 to September 30; activity usually starts in late June or early July, peaks in July and August, and lingers into September.
- Expect afternoon storms, flash floods in washes and low crossings, and dust storms in southern Arizona. Ride desert miles in the morning. Never cross a flooded wash.

## Wind

- Strong crosswinds are tiring and risky on a loaded motorcycle; gusts are worse than steady wind. As a rough guide, sustained winds above about 25 mph or gusts above about 40 mph call for a rethink.
- Watch ridge crests, bridges, canyon mouths, and open plains. I-80 in southern Wyoming is known for high-wind closures to light, high-profile vehicles; a motorcycle is lighter than anything on those lists, so treat a high-wind warning there as a stop sign.
- Headwind also costs fuel range.

## Fog

- Coastal fog (California Highway 1, the Oregon coast) is most common on summer mornings. Valley and mountain fog peak at dawn in fall. Fog plus a twisty road is a reason to start later.

## Dusk, dawn, and wildlife

- Deer collisions peak at dusk and dawn, on rural roads, and in October through December; elk are larger and similar. Dusk is far worse than full daylight.
- Plan to arrive 30–60 minutes before sunset, and avoid riding unfamiliar rural roads at night, especially in fall ranch and forest country.
- In late fall and winter, usable daylight can be under 10 hours; shrink day lengths to fit. Civil twilight, not sunset, is the practical end of a motorcycle day.

## Sun glare

- Riding west near sunset or east near sunrise puts the sun in the rider's eyes. In winter the sun stays low, so east-west roads can be bad for hours. Order the day to avoid it where possible.

## Seasonal mountain roads

Typical windows only. Openings slip in big snow years, roads can close again after an early or late storm, and "open" can mean partly open. Always check the official source before riding.

- Going-to-the-Sun Road (Glacier, Montana): since about 2007, full opening has usually come between mid-June and mid-July, and several years closed in September. Plan as if July 1 to late September is the dependable window.
- Trail Ridge Road (Rocky Mountain National Park, Colorado): typically late May to mid or late October.
- Beartooth Highway (US-212, Montana and Wyoming): typically Memorial Day weekend to mid-October; the two states' sides can differ.
- Independence Pass (CO-82, Colorado): typically late May to late October or mid-November.
- Mount Blue Sky Road (CO-5, Colorado): typically Memorial Day; the summit section usually closes after Labor Day.
- Tioga Road (CA-120, Yosemite): typically late May or June to November, with a very wide range (opening as late as July 22 in 2023).
- Sonora Pass (CA-108) and Ebbetts Pass (CA-4): typically late May to November.
- North Cascades Highway (WA-20): typically early May to mid-November or December.

## Best seasons by region

General riding-press consensus, lower confidence than the rest of this file.

- Sierra Nevada passes: late June to early October; September is often best.
- Northern Rockies (Glacier, Beartooth, Yellowstone): July to mid-September.
- Colorado Rockies: mid-June to late September, watching afternoon storms.
- Utah and Arizona canyon country: spring and fall; summer is extreme heat at low elevation.
- Pacific Northwest: July and August are most reliable.
- Appalachians: spring, and late September to October for color; high sections can close for ice in winter.
- Southern deserts, Texas Hill Country, and Florida: winter.
