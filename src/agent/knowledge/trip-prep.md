# Trip prep

How to use this: a preparation checklist to offer riders before a multi-day trip. It is practice, not regulation; adapt it to the group and the vehicle.

## Packing list

- Keep a written packing list for body and bike. Start it days or weeks ahead, keep refining it, cross off what turns out to be unnecessary, and keep it for the next trip.
- Talk with the group and consolidate shared items—tools, first aid, chain lube—so the group does not carry twelve cans of chain lube and no bandages.

## Pack light

- Unless the trip goes somewhere truly remote, the group can stop in a town and buy what it forgot (within reason—not four times a day). Do not pack your entire life.
- Overfull bags take longer to pack every morning, and more time packing is less time riding. Weight also affects handling and fuel range.

## Bike prep

- Check the chain, the tread left on both tires, and the brake pads. Look up the last oil change. Make sure the bike is covered for the full distance of the trip, or have a plan to change tires or oil along the way.
- Fix problems and replace consumables, but make no major modifications before a trip. No new lighting systems, no ECU remap the night before. New gadgets can fail, and when one rider has trouble, the whole group has trouble.
- Know the bike: distance to reserve and distance to empty; whether it is carbureted and how it behaves at high altitude; whether that noise at idle is normal.

## Shakedown ride

- At least a couple of weeks before the trip, ride local roads for an hour or two. Listen for rattles and noises and make sure everything feels right, while there is still time to order parts and fix anything wonky.
- Check that you feel safe, comfortable, and confident. A rider having close calls around town is not ready to ride in a group for thousands of miles.

## Know your body

- Has the rider been riding much lately? Are they ready to ride all day, every day, for several days running?
- Are they coming down with something, or carrying new aches or pains that would make long days unsafe? Better to say so before the trip than on day three.

## Always carry

- Spare keys: an extra ignition key, plus spares for any disc lock, bike lock, or lockable luggage, kept on the rider's person where they cannot be lost or left behind (a zipped jacket pocket, not the bike). Dropping the only key down a storm drain in the middle of nowhere ends a trip.
- Papers: a current driver's license with the right motorcycle endorsement, proof of insurance, and registration. For Mexico or Canada, a valid passport (see the borders guide).
- Water: riders sweat more than they notice because the wind dries it. Carry and drink more than seems necessary; on hot days replace electrolytes. Drink-mix packets are cheaper and lighter than buying sports drinks at every stop.
- Snacks: the group may stop for breakfast or lunch but probably not both, and may skip a meal to make up time. Carry high-protein snacks (bars, trail mix, jerky). Riders with medical or dietary needs—diabetic, kosher, vegan, gluten-free, and so on—carry food that works for them; not every truck stop has options at every hour.
- Layers and rain gear: nights get cold even in hot regions, and a day over passes and through valleys can cross several climates. Pack for rain. For any chance of extended riding below about 50°F, a heated vest is worth it.
- Medications in their original labeled bottles, with the name matching the rider's ID.

## General kit worth suggesting

Not from the owner's guide; common touring practice, offered as suggestions.

- A tire plug kit and a small inflator or CO2 cartridges, and the knowledge to use them.
- A basic tool kit that fits the bike's fasteners, zip ties, tape, and a flashlight.
- A first aid kit (shared across the group).
- Offline maps and the route saved on the phone or GPS, since coverage disappears on rural roads.
- Phone charging on the bike.

## Sharing is caring

- Trade emergency contact information with the group before leaving, so someone can reach a rider's partner or family if the rider cannot.
- Write important phone numbers and information on paper as well, in case a phone dies, breaks, or is lost.
- Share live location with the group for the length of the trip (for example, Google Maps location sharing, which can be set to expire on its own), in case anyone gets separated.

## Laws along the way

- Laws change at city, county, state, and national lines—helmets, lane filtering, speed limits, and what can legally be carried. Read up on the places the route passes through; see the borders and laws guide.
- Ride so as not to give police a reason to stop or search. Current, complete paperwork matters; reckless riding can mean impound.
- Crossing a border, be even more careful: prescriptions in original labeled bottles, and check rules on weapons, including pepper spray and bear spray.

## Cars and bicycles

- Cars: tires, brakes, fluids, and a spare or repair kit; the same papers, water, and emergency contacts apply. A support vehicle should carry the group's shared spares and know every stop.
- Bicycles: tubes or a tubeless repair kit, a pump, a multi-tool with a chain tool, spare brake pads on long trips, a tune-up before departure, and a shakedown ride fully loaded. Water capacity matters more than almost anything else on remote routes.
