# Group riding

How to use this: practice and etiquette for planning a group ride. These are norms riders agree on, not laws; the group's own agreements always win.

## Set expectations before the trip

- The most important thing a group can do is agree, at home, on the route, the start, stop, and break schedule, the destinations, and the priorities. Ambiguity turns into conflict on the road when everyone is tired, hot, and less willing to compromise.
- Riders differ wildly. Some want 14-hour days; some think 100 miles is plenty and want to stop at noon. Neither is wrong; they just need to know before they leave.
- Agree on KSU (kickstands up) time and the day's stop time in advance. Disagreements about either are settled while planning, not at the motel at 7 am.
- If anything is unclear, talk about it. If it seems clear, talk about it again anyway.
- When the assistant is helping plan a group trip, surface these questions: how long a day, what time to leave, how many breaks, which meals, booked lodging or winging it, and who leads.

## Ride your own ride

- Nobody rides over their head or their health to keep up with the pack. Riders travel at different paces and do not always feel their best. A rider who crashes trying to keep up makes everyone's trip worse.
- A rider not feeling 100% tells the leader, then slows down and drops back. The leader never takes a turn without stopping to wait, so a rider who falls behind keeps going straight at intersections until they catch up.
- A rider who feels great and wants to go ahead either asks to lead or finds out where the next turn is first, then goes.
- Faster riders are not pestered to slow down, and slower riders are never pressured to speed up.

## Regroup at the next stop

- Mixed pace is solved by agreeing on the next rest or gas stop and meeting there. Everyone rides at their comfortable pace; fast riders get a few extra minutes of rest while the others catch up.
- Prefer regrouping at planned stops over stopping at every turn. Good regroup points have room to park the whole group off the road and clear sight lines: gas stations, large pull-offs, a town square. Avoid narrow shoulders and spots just past a blind corner.
- For larger groups or very different paces, splitting into sub-groups that meet at agreed stops works better than one long, accordioning line.

## Leader and sweep

- The leader sets the pace and route, and should share turns and destinations openly; nobody should be riding blind.
- The sweep is an experienced rider at the back who stays with anyone who stops, breaks down, or falls behind. Nobody leaves a downed or broken-down rider alone.
- Each sub-group gets its own leader and sweep.
- The leader on twisty roads keeps the pace moderate; a fast leader makes the back of the group accordion and speed to catch up, which is where trouble happens.

## Formation and spacing

- On open roads, a staggered formation is common: about 2 seconds to the rider directly ahead in the same track and about 1 second to the rider diagonally ahead.
- In twisty roads, poor visibility, or bad surfaces, ride single file with more space so each rider can use the whole lane.
- Keep a comfortable buffer. Riding too close to keep the group tight is a common cause of group crashes.
- Groups much larger than about 5–7 bikes become hard to manage; split them.

## Be predictable

- No sudden, unsignaled turns or lane changes. Smooth, logical movements let everyone behind anticipate.
- Signal fuel and stops early so the riders behind can follow.
- Agree on a few hand signals (stop, fuel, slow, hazard, turn) or use intercoms if the group has them.

## Passing in the twisties

- On a twisty road, a safe gap past a slow vehicle often fits only one or two bikes. The third, fourth, or fifth rider does not force a late pass into a blind corner or over a blind crest.
- Wait for the next safe opportunity, however long it takes. The group will not leave anyone behind.

## Separation plan

- Everyone should be able to get to the day's destination alone: the route and the night's stop are shared in advance, ideally loaded on each rider's phone or GPS and kept offline.
- Share live location with the group for the length of the trip (for example, Google Maps location sharing, which expires on its own).
- If someone goes missing: the leader pulls over somewhere visible and waits, or the group waits at the next agreed stop; the lost rider does not speed to catch up.
- Everyone carries the others' phone numbers and emergency contacts, including on paper in case a phone dies.
- Cell coverage disappears on many rural roads, so a plan that depends on calling each other is not a plan.

## Fuel in a group

- The shortest tank sets the stops, and when one fills, everyone fills. See the fuel guide.

## Speak up

Complaining about being tired, hot, or sore is fine and normal—everyone is. But any of these is a safety issue a rider must say out loud, right away:

- Feeling oddly cold when it is hot out, or oddly hot when it is cold.
- Dizziness, light-headedness, nausea, or disorientation.
- Genuinely struggling to stay awake ("I'm falling asleep," not "I'm tired").
- Sharp, unusual pain that affects the ability to ride ("I need a doctor," not "my back is sore").

The group stops for any of these.

## Cars and bicycles in a group

- Cars in convoy: the same principles apply—agree on stops, share the route, meet at the next stop rather than chasing each other through traffic. A support vehicle carrying luggage or spares should know every stop and the night's destination.
- Mixed motorcycles and cars: the motorcycles' limits (weather, fatigue, fuel range) usually set the plan.
- Bicycles: ride single file where traffic or shoulders demand it, regroup at the top of climbs and at junctions, and agree on a pace or split by pace. Distances between regroup points are much shorter than for motorcycles.
