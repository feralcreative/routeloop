# Pacing

How to use this: rules of thumb for sizing days, not limits. The group's own stated preferences always outrank these numbers, and no figure here comes from a controlled study.

## The first rule: ask, then plan by time

- Before proposing day lengths, find out what this group means by a good day. Some riders happily ride 14-hour days with no breaks; others think 100 miles is a long ride and want to be by the pool by noon. Both are normal. If the planner has not said, ask once rather than assume.
- Plan days in hours, not miles. A hundred miles of interstate and a hundred miles of mountain two-lane are different days. Convert each stretch to riding time at a realistic average speed, then add stops.
- Plan for the slowest rider and the smallest tank in the group, never the fastest or the average.

## Daily distance by style (motorcycle)

Sources disagree by a wide margin; give a range and say it depends on the riders, bikes, and roads.

- Relaxed, sightseeing-heavy touring: about 150–200 miles a day, less with a new pillion.
- Group riding on twisty roads: about 180–220 miles, roughly 5 hours in the saddle and 8–9 hours door to door once stops are added.
- Mixed back roads and highway: about 250–350 miles is a full day for most groups.
- Transit days on the slab: experienced riders cover 400–500 miles or more, but interstate is the most tiring riding per hour (wind blast, constant attention), so a long slab day is not an easy day.
- Gravel or adventure routes: plan 5–6 hours of riding; 7 or more is ambitious. Distance depends entirely on surface—100 paved mountain miles can take two hours while a short rocky trail can eat the whole day.
- Cruisers, small bikes, and riders out of practice often top out lower; a rider's own longest comfortable day is the best guide. Do not schedule a run of 500-mile days for someone whose best is 300.

## Average speeds to plan with

Moving averages, before stops. Treat them as starting points.

- Interstate and main highways: about 55–62 mph.
- Twisty two-lane with a group: about 40–45 mph.
- Tight gravel: about 30 mph. Rough dirt: 20–25 mph or less.
- Elapsed time: multiply riding time by roughly 1.5 for a lean day and up to 2 for a sociable one (breaks, fuel, meals, photos, and a group's slower restarts).
- Riding well above traffic speed costs fuel and tires you out faster; it rarely saves as much time as it feels like it does.

## Breaks

- Motorcycles need breaks more often than cars: roughly every hour to every 2 hours, or about every 70–100 miles. Larger groups trend toward the shorter end.
- Fuel stops usually double as breaks. A group fuel stop takes 20–40 minutes, not the 10 minutes it takes one rider; budget for it.
- Keep breaks short and active: walk, stretch, drink water. Long sitting breaks make the restart harder.
- Fatigue signs: drifting in corners, speeding up and slowing down without reason, missing turns, indecision, nodding. If a rider needs caffeine just to stay alert, the day should end soon. "I'm falling asleep" is a stop now, not at the next town.

## Meals

- Plan breakfast or lunch, not both. A sit-down meal for a group easily costs an hour; two of them cut a day short. Riders carry high-protein snacks (bars, trail mix, jerky) to bridge the gap.
- Once in a while a meal gets skipped to make up time. Say so in the plan rather than surprising people.
- Small-town cafes in the rural West and Midwest often close by early afternoon and on Sundays or Mondays; check hours for the day of travel before relying on one.
- A group of 8 or more slows every restaurant stop; counter service or calling ahead helps.

## KSU and stop times, agreed up front

- KSU ("kickstands up") is a departure time the group agrees on before the trip, not on the road. KSU means packed, on the bike, helmet and gloves on, engine running—not "almost ready."
- Agree the stop time too. A rider who thinks there is one hour left and another who wants a few hundred more miles will have a bad evening.
- If someone thinks the agreed time is too early or too late, that conversation happens while planning at home. On the road, nobody drags it out and nobody rushes others ahead of the agreed time.
- Plan to arrive at the night's stop 30–60 minutes before sunset, earlier in deer country and in fall. Arrival after dark is a planning failure to flag, not a detail.

## Group size

- Every added rider adds time at every stop: fuel, restrooms, ordering, and getting moving again.
- Around 5–7 bikes is the common comfortable maximum for one group; above that, suggest splitting into sub-groups, each with its own leader and sweep, meeting at agreed stops.

## Long trips and fatigue

- Mileage comfort drops as a trip goes on. One long-distance riding source puts a rider on day 7 at about two thirds of what they would happily do on day 1 or 2; treat that as a rough shape, not a formula.
- Do not open a trip with the longest days; build up. Many tourers like getting a big chunk done early in a day, but a long first day after a long drive or poor sleep is a common mistake.
- Build slack: a short, easy day about every third day helps a group absorb weather, breakdowns, and late starts. Some planners prefer a short day after every 2–3 hard ones; either works.
- Rest days: one full day off per week or so on long trips is common advice; bicycle tourers are advised at least one rest day per 10 days. Bad weather or extreme heat is a fine reason to take one early.
- Flag runs of three or more long days back to back, and offer a cuttable loop or shortcut on long days so the group can trim without replanning.

## Cars and bicycles

- Cars: break about every 2 hours or 100 miles. Around 8 hours of driving is where most people start splitting a day; 500 miles is roughly 8–9 hours of driving. A car can comfortably cover more than a motorcycle group, but a mixed group moves at the bikes' pace.
- Bicycles (loaded touring on pavement): about 50–65 miles a day depending on load, about half that or less on dirt and mountain-bike routes. Beginners often start at 25–45 miles. Loaded touring speed is roughly 10–14 mph moving. A rider is ready when they can do back-to-back days at the planned length and feel they could do a third.

## When uncertain

If the group has not stated preferences, propose a moderate plan (about 200–300 miles, 6–8 hours elapsed for a motorcycle group on mixed roads), name the assumption, and ask whether they want more or less.
