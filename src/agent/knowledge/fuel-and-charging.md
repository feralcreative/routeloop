# Fuel and charging

How to use this: rules of thumb for spacing fuel, charging, and water stops. Never state that a specific station exists, is open, or sells a given grade without checking a live source; small stations close and change hours.

## The group's range is the shortest tank

- The bike with the shortest range sets every fuel stop. Plan around that bike, not the average.
- Use real-world range, not the spec sheet. Each rider should know distance to reserve and distance to empty, from their worst recent tank (headwind, speed, load), not their best.
- When one fills, everyone fills. A big-tank bike that skips a stop will need fuel 20 miles after the short-tank bike's next stop, and the group stops twice.
- Start every day full. Fueling the night before saves the first stop of the morning.
- Riders who know they have the smallest tanks should say so before the trip, and in a group they can ride near the front and call fuel.

## Margin

- Reserve is for emergencies, not a plan. On most motorcycles reserve is under a gallon.
- Plan fuel stops at about 70–80% of the shortest real-world range, so the group arrives with roughly 20–30% left. On a 180-mile range, a 175-mile gap is too tight.
- In remote country, many riders fill whenever the tank is below half and a station appears; that is a sensible habit where stations are far apart and hours are short.
- Small-town stations often close early (5–8 pm), close on Sundays, run out of fuel, or have card readers that fail. Fill at the last reliable station before a known gap even if the tank is half full.

## Group fuel stops

- A group fuel stop takes 20–40 minutes once you count queuing at the pumps, paying, restrooms, and getting everyone moving. One rider takes 10. No good source puts a number on time per rider; plan conservatively.
- Choose stations with room for the whole group and several pumps. Ten or twelve bikes can block a small station.
- Pairing a fuel stop with a break or meal hides most of its cost.
- Signal the stop early so the riders behind know.

## What costs range

All of these are directional; the size of the effect varies by vehicle and should be treated as an estimate.

- Speed: the biggest single factor above about 60 mph, because drag rises steeply. Rider reports suggest 75–80 mph can cost 15–25% compared with 65.
- Headwind: works like extra speed. A strong headwind can cost a large share of range; a long day into the wind across open country deserves a shorter planned gap.
- Load: luggage, camping gear, and a passenger cost some range.
- Cold: somewhat worse economy for gas vehicles; much worse range for electric ones (below).
- Altitude: fuel-injected bikes adjust; carbureted bikes can run rich and lose economy and power at high elevation. Ask whether any bike is carbureted before routing a long high-altitude stretch.
- Octane: high-elevation stations often sell 85 as regular. A bike that needs 91 or higher needs a premium stop planned; follow the owner's manual minimum.

## Long gaps and how to handle them

- Identify the longest gap between reliable stations on each day and compare it with the shortest range at 70–80%.
- If the gap is too long: re-route through a fuel town, carry approved auxiliary fuel on the short-tank bikes (only they need it), or split the group so long-range bikes are not held back.
- Auxiliary fuel goes in an approved container, mounted securely and low; loose cans affect handling and are a fire risk.
- Corridors known for long gaps or limited hours, to check carefully rather than to quote numbers from: US-50 across Nevada; US-50 and nearby routes between Ely, Nevada, and Delta, Utah; Death Valley and the roads into it; Baja California Highway 1, especially between El Rosario and Guerrero Negro; eastern Oregon; central and southern Utah back roads; the Owyhee region. Gap lengths quoted online vary because stations open and close.
- In Mexico, carry cash; many remote stations take cash only, and in some stretches fuel is sold from barrels at a premium.

## Electric motorcycles

- Highway range is short: plan around 70–80 miles at interstate speed for current mainstream models, more at lower speeds. Ask for the rider's real-world number.
- Charging speed depends on the model. Some support DC fast charging (one model claims 0–80% in about 40 minutes); many charge only at Level 2 or slower, and a household outlet can take most of a day. Check the specific bike before assuming a stop length.
- Cold weather can cut range by a fifth or more.
- A 300-mile day on an electric bike can mean five or more charging stops of 40–60 minutes. In a mixed group, this reshapes the whole day; say so plainly and offer to plan stops at chargers with food nearby.
- Connector type matters (CCS, J1772, NACS); match it to the bike.

## Electric cars

- Charge in the fast part of the curve: arrive around 10–20%, leave around 80%. Above 80% charging slows sharply; 10–80% takes about as long as 80–100%.
- On highway trips, plan on about 70–80% of rated range; in cold weather, roughly 55–65%. Never plan to arrive at a charger below about 10%.
- Prefer sites with several chargers and a backup site within range; single-charger sites are a risk. Reliability check-ins on charger apps are worth reading.
- A 20–40 minute charge fits a meal stop; pick chargers with food within walking distance.

## Bicycles: water and food, not fuel

- The gap that matters is between water and food sources, not fuel.
- In heat, about 1 liter of water per hour of riding is a common starting point; long desert gaps (some stretches of US-50 in Nevada have 70–85 miles with no services) mean carrying 4–6 liters or more plus food for the gap and a spare day's worth.
- Flag any gap over about 50 miles without water and ask how much the rider can carry.
- Start early to ride long gaps before afternoon heat.

## Cars

- Cars have longer range and rarely set the stops in a mixed group, but a car pulling a trailer, or a support vehicle, may have a shorter range than expected; ask.
